# Writing wishes on paper and hanging them up: the Chinese traditions and their neighbours

As at 1 October 2026. Prepared for personal cultural research ahead of the move to Ireland. Covers the Chinese practices of writing a prayer or wish on paper and hanging it, what they mean, the rituals that sit in the same family, and a walkthrough for doing a respectful version at home. Sources are numbered in square brackets and listed at the end, each marked with how it was accessed.

## The answer in brief

There is no single Chinese name for "writing a wish and hanging it up" because it is a family of practices rather than one rite. Three forms are the core of it.

1. The wishing tree (許願樹, xǔyuàn shù). The famous site is Lam Tsuen in Hong Kong's New Territories. You buy a wishing placard (寶牒, Cantonese bou2 dip6, Mandarin bǎodié), a sheet of red and yellow joss paper, write your name, date of birth and wish on it, tie it to an orange (today a plastic one) and throw it up into a banyan tree. If it catches on a branch the wish is accepted, and the higher it lodges the better. If it falls, folk belief says the wish was too greedy; one account gives you three tries before you should scale it down [1][2][3][4].

2. The temple wish card or plaque (祈福牌 qífú pái, "blessing-seeking tag", or 許願牌 xǔyuàn pái, "wish-making tag"). A red wooden or card tag on which you write your name and wish, then hang on a rack, board or tree beside the altar. Found in Buddhist and Taoist temples across China and Taiwan, from Jiming Temple in Nanjing to Beigang Chaotian Temple in Taiwan [5][6][7].

3. The wish ribbon (祈福帶 or 許願帶). A red ribbon written on and tied to a tree. Some temples moved from oranges to ribbons because the fruit rotted; the rule that higher is better carries over [8][9][10].

What it means comes down to five ideas that run through all three forms, and through every neighbouring ritual below.

- A wish is a petition to an official. In Chinese folk religion, Taoism and lay Buddhism, you address a deity the way you would address a magistrate. You give your full name, birth date and address so the god can find you, and you state the request precisely. The written placard or card is the lay version of the Taoist shūwén (疏文), a formal petition that a priest reads aloud and then burns so that it is delivered to heaven [11][12][13][14].
- A wish is a vow. Making a wish is xǔyuàn (許願, "to promise a wish"). If it is granted you owe a return, huányuàn (還願, "to repay the vow"): you go back, give thanks and make an offering. The pattern is ancient; the search results describe a "spring prayer, autumn repayment" cycle to the god of the soil as its root [14][15][5].
- Trees are addressed because they are inhabited. In south China and Taiwan old banyans and camphors are seen as the homes of earth gods, or as tree gods in their own right (大樹公, Dàshù gōng, "great tree lord"). Villagers drape red cloth on the trunk, build shrines at the base and put children under the tree's protection. The original Lam Tsuen wishing tree was a camphor holding a tablet to Pak Kung, the Hakka name for the earth god [16][17][18][19].
- Red and height. Red is luck and joy and repels harm. Height is nearness to heaven and visibility to the gods [8][9][23].
- Paper, smoke and air carry the message. The same logic drives joss-paper burning, sky lanterns and prayer flags: the medium rises, so the message does.

## The wishing tree at Lam Tsuen: the full account

Lam Tsuen has been settled since the Song dynasty and its Tin Hau temple was built around 1768 to 1771 under the Qianlong emperor [19][20]. The wishing custom was not a village tradition. In a 2000 Chinese University of Hong Kong piece, a Hong Kong Tourist Association spokeswoman said a dying fisherman seeking a miracle brought it to the village about forty years earlier, and that fishermen passing through at Lunar New Year threw joss paper at the Pak Kung trees for luck and protection [18] [single source]. A Hong Kong heritage site calls it "a Taoist custom handed down" in which fishermen wrote their names and birth dates on placards and hung them on trees beside the temple to pray for good catches [3] [single source].

The tree itself has been replaced several times. The first, a camphor beside the Tin Hau temple, burned on the first day of the Lunar New Year in 1998; its replacement also burned, and the custom moved to the two banyans that stand today [1][3][19]. A 1998 TVB drama made the site famous across Hong Kong [2][21], and in the 1990s the stones once used as weights were replaced by oranges [21] [single source]. On 12 February 2005 a branch overloaded with placards gave way and injured two people, and throwing was banned [1][2]. Wooden racks were installed, and in 2009 the Lam Tsuen Rural Committee added a plastic tree about fifteen feet tall with artificial oranges so that the throw could continue without harming the banyans [2][22].

The Lam Tsuen Well-Wishing Festival now runs from the first to the fifteenth day of the lunar year. A placard with its plastic mandarin costs about HK$25. Local lore assigns the smaller banyan to wishes for pregnancy and marriage and the larger to study, career, wealth and health [2][4][22].

## How to do it yourself

Three honest ways to engage. Visit a temple or wishing site and take part as a guest, following the house steps. Do a home version that keeps the logic: a named written petition, hung high, repaid when granted. Or treat it as decoration, which is fine, as long as you know that is what you are doing. The walkthrough below is the home version, modelled on the Lam Tsuen placard and the temple card.

### Step 1. Choose the moment

The traditional window is the Lunar New Year season, from New Year's Day to the Lantern Festival. In 2027 that is Saturday 6 February to Saturday 20 February [58]. A threshold of your own also fits the spirit of the practice: the move, a birthday, the first night in a new house.

### Step 2. Choose the frame

A living tree in the garden works if you tie gently and take the cards down afterwards. A bare branch set in a heavy pot indoors works just as well, and so does a wall-mounted rack or board. Lam Tsuen itself moved to a rack and an artificial tree in 2009, so there is precedent for the substitute [2][22]. Never throw weighted objects into a living tree; that is what broke the branch in 2005.

### Step 3. Gather the materials

- Red or gold paper cut to roughly postcard size, or small red card tags with a hole punched at the top. Joss-paper shops and East Asian supermarkets sell printed placards; plain red card is fine.
- Red ribbon or red cotton string.
- A brush pen, or a black or gold marker.
- Incense, three sticks per person, optional. Three stands for heaven, earth and humanity [14].
- One orange or mandarin per person as an offering to set at the base, not to throw.

### Step 4. Write the placard

Write your full name, your date of birth and the wish, one wish per card. Be specific and be modest; the folk rule that a greedy wish falls out of the tree is the tradition's own guidance on scope [4]. Lam Tsuen groups wishes into health, family peace, career, study, love and children [4][22]. The standard four-character blessings used on fai chun and placards include 身體健康 (good health), 出入平安 (safety coming and going), 心想事成 (may what the heart wishes come true), 學業進步 (progress in study), 生意興隆 (flourishing business) and 萬事如意 (success in all things) [25][26]. Writing in English is fine; the deity reads intent, and the point is that the wish is named and signed.

Buddhist temples encourage one more thing: write at least one card for the wellbeing of others, not only your own. The 2023 study of wish-cards in Chinese temples calls this the bodhisattva stance, and it is what turns a wish into a vow [5].

### Step 5. Say it aloud

Light the incense if you are using it. Hold the card, face the tree or rack, and state your name, your birth date, where you live and the wish, out loud. Bow three times. Temple etiquette across Taiwan and Hong Kong is explicit that the spoken identification matters as much as the written one [13][14][59].

### Step 6. Hang it

Tie the ribbon or string through the card and place it as high as you comfortably can. If you want the throw, tie the card to something light (a plastic mandarin, a pine cone) and toss it at the rack or the artificial branch, three tries; if it will not catch, the tradition says the wish wants scaling down [4].

### Step 7. Leave it up through the season

Keep the cards up at least until the Lantern Festival. Set the oranges at the base on the first day and eat them when the season ends.

### Step 8. Repay the vow

This is the step outsiders skip and the one that defines the practice. When a wish comes true, go back to the spot, say thanks aloud, and make a small offering: fruit, a donation to a cause in the area of the wish, or a kindness done in its name. At Xiahai temple a married couple brings wedding sweets back to Yue Lao [41][42]; at Buddhist temples people return with incense, a copied sutra or a gift to the temple [14].

### Step 9. Retire the cards

Take them down at the end of the year or the season. The traditional transmission is burning, done safely outdoors in a fire-safe container. Keeping them in a box is an acceptable modern alternative. Do not bin them casually, and hang fresh ones the next year, as households do with couplets and talismans [36][23].

### Variants

- The doorway version. Buy or write fai chun and paste them on the front door on New Year's Eve: the horizontal scroll above, the opening line on the right as you face the door and the answering line on the left (classical Chinese reads right to left), and an upside-down 福 on the inside of the door [23][27].
- The Tanabata version. On 7 July, or on Qixi in August, write wishes on paper strips in the five colours (green, red, yellow, white, purple) and hang them on a bamboo branch [50][51].
- The ribbon version. Write along a length of red cotton ribbon and tie it to a branch [8][9].
- The Irish bridge. At a holy well with a rag tree, use a strip of biodegradable cloth, dip it in the well and tie it on with a prayer. The tradition requires the rag to rot, so synthetic ribbon defeats it [55][56].

### If you visit a temple or wishing site

Buy the card or placard from the temple itself. Greet the main deity first, then the one you are petitioning. State your name, birth date and address. Hang the card where the temple indicates. Do not photograph other people's cards up close, and go back to give thanks if the wish lands [13][14][59].

### Safety and respect

- Sky lanterns in Ireland need prior permission from the Irish Aviation Authority, and the Coast Guard has asked the public to stop releasing them because they trigger false flare alerts [32]. Write the wish on a paper lantern and keep it as a lamp instead.
- Never throw fruit or weights at a living tree, and use cotton string rather than plastic ribbon on live branches so nothing girdles them; remove everything when the season ends.
- In Ireland a lone hawthorn is a fairy tree in local lore. Tying to one is a tradition in its own right at holy wells, and a trespass anywhere else; ask before you tie.
- Yellow paper with red script is the talisman register and belongs to a consecrated rite. For a home wish, use red or gold paper and black or gold ink.

## Addendum, 1 October 2026: the household altar, the hanging censer and the rack

Added after the decision to build a home version: cards addressed to the universe, a deconstructed temple rack across the ceiling, a weekly act of gratitude incense or candle at an altar, and a burn with thanks at Lunar New Year.

### What a Chinese home altar is made of

The Daoist teaching sources agree on five essentials: an incense burner (香爐 xiānglú, a bowl of ash or sand that the sticks stand in), an offering plate, a candle or oil lamp, a water cup, and the object of veneration, which can be a deity image or simply the written character for the Way. The burner is the oldest object type on the altar and sits front and centre. Candles come as one (the heart-light) or a pair (yin and yang), red by tradition, never five. In front of the burner go up to three small cups holding, left to right, water, rice and tea. The water is changed every morning and is there to balance the fire of the lamp [60][61][62].

Modern households commonly replace the oil lamp with a pair of red electric altar candles, which are kept lit through major observances and are sold as standard altar supplies [65][76]. Fruit (oranges and apples are the usual) and fresh flowers rotate on the plate; they are not required daily but are expected on the first and fifteenth of the lunar month [60][64][65].

### Placement rules

The altar stands against a solid wall at chest or eye height, never below the waist. It faces the main door or south, and never faces a toilet, a kitchen stove, a bedroom door or a rubbish area. It is not placed under a beam or a staircase [62][63]. The bedroom itself is avoided in the traditional view: a deity image should not oversee sleep and intimacy, a statue below eye level or at the foot of the bed is a taboo, and feng shui writers add that a yang object in a yin room disturbs sleep [71][72]. All of these rules concern an altar that holds a deity image. An altar with no image is a different case, below.

### The rhythm of tending

Incense is offered morning and evening, first to the gods and then to the ancestors, and the water is refreshed each morning. The fuller offering (food, fruit, flowers, spirit money) comes on the first and fifteenth of each lunar month, which fall roughly on the new and full moon [60][64][65]. The point for a home version is that the tradition's own cadence is a small daily act plus a larger fortnightly one, so a fixed weekly act with spontaneous moments sits comfortably inside it.

### How incense is offered

Three sticks is the standard, read as heaven, earth and humanity, or as Buddha, Dharma and Sangha. Four is never used because the word sounds like death. Light all three together from a candle or lamp, never blow the flame out (blowing is treated like spitting on the offering; wave the sticks until only embers remain), hold them at brow height with the right hand and the left hand wrapped around it, make the request silently, bow with eyes closed, then plant them in the burner: the first in the centre, the second to the left, the third to the right [59][66][67]. Sandalwood (檀香) joss sticks are the everyday altar incense; agarwood (沉香) is the costly one. Thin, low-smoke sandalwood sticks of about thirty minutes' burn are what the suppliers sell for daily home use [75].

### The precedent for an altar with no image, hung from above

Hoklo Taiwanese households hang a censer to Tiangong, the Lord of Heaven, from the middle of the hall beam. It is suspended on four chains, and the Jade Emperor temples venerate the censer alone, with no statue at all [68][69]. This is the closest Chinese precedent for what you described: an address to heaven itself, hung from the ceiling, with no deity image. It also sidesteps every bedroom taboo above, since those turn on a deity image overlooking the room.

### How the rack is built

The wish tag (祈福牌) is a red wooden plaque, often with a red tassel, carrying carved or written text, hung by a red cord on wooden frames or "blessing walls" at temples, old towns, scenic sites and ancient trees. Common occasions named are Qingming, the gaokao and New Year wishes [70]. At Lam Tsuen the post-2005 solution is wooden racks beside the tree [1][2]. Nothing in the sources prescribes the frame; the constant is the red cord, the rows, and the accumulation.

### Thanks when a wish is granted

Return to the altar or temple with incense and thanks; make a donation or do a charitable act in the deity's name; offer fruit, tea, flowers or food at home. The sources describe this as an exchange of gratitude rather than a payment [14][74].

### Incense and the animals

Veterinary and pet-health sources are consistent: incense smoke causes sneezing, congestion, watery eyes and wheezing in cats, dogs are also affected, and the advice is to burn only in a ventilated room the cat does not use, in short sessions, with natural rather than synthetic sticks [73]. Scented candles carry the same warning. The red electric altar candle is the pet-safe default, with real incense reserved for a ventilated room with the cats shut out, or for the outdoor burn at New Year.
