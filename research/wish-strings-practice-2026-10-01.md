# The wish strings: a home practice

As at 1 October 2026. A private practice for Augustus, built from the research in `chinese-wish-writing-traditions-2026-10-01.md`. It borrows the form of the temple wish rack and the Tiangong hall censer from Chinese folk practice, the string-and-air form from Tibetan prayer flags, and the idea of feeding a wish with attention from Western intention practice. It is inspired by these and is not any of them.

## Decisions already made

- Cards are addressed to the universe, as direct asks, not mantras.
- The rack is deconstructed across the ceiling of one room, private, for Augustus and Kyle only.
- A fixed weekly act at a small altar, with spontaneous moments whenever a card catches the eye.
- Gratitude by incense or candle at the altar.
- A gift of thanks when a wish is fulfilled.
- All cards burn with thanks at Lunar New Year (6 February 2027, then annually).

## The three parts

### 1. The strings

Red cotton cord run across the ceiling in parallel lines or a loose web, fixed at the walls with small hooks. Hang them high enough that Rocki and Izzi cannot reach a dangling card from any piece of furniture, and keep the lowest card above head height. If the room has a window, let at least one line pass near it so the cards move in the air; in the prayer-flag logic, that movement is the blessing being carried.

Each card hangs on its own short red cord from a tiny S-hook or a slip knot, so a card can be added or taken down without touching the line. Hooks and cord are the whole mechanism; the temple racks prescribe nothing else.

### 2. The cards

Red or gold card, roughly 6 by 10 cm, hole punched at the top, a red tassel if you like the look. Black or gold pen. One wish per card, written at the moment the feeling arrives, in this order:

1. Your full name.
2. The date.
3. The wish, as a direct ask, specific and modest.

Write in English. Add a Chinese blessing if it fits (身體健康 good health, 出入平安 safety coming and going, 心想事成 may what the heart wishes come true, 萬事如意 success in all things), but it is not required. Write at least one card for someone else's good each season; the Buddhist temples treat that as the thing that turns a wish into a vow.

Hang a new card the moment it is written, say it aloud once facing the altar, then go on with the day. The spoken naming is the lodging of the petition.

### 3. The altar

Modelled on the Tiangong hall censer: an address to heaven with no deity image. That form also sidesteps the traditional rule against altars in bedrooms, which turns on a deity image overlooking the room.

What goes on it, left to right as you face it:

- One cup of fresh water, changed each morning if you pass it, at least weekly.
- The incense burner front and centre: a small bowl of clean sand or ash, or a stick holder.
- A pair of red electric altar candles, one at each end, left on through the weekly act and through the New Year season.
- A small plate for fruit on the weekly act and for the gift of thanks.

Where: a shelf or small cabinet against a solid wall, at chest height or higher, not under a beam, not facing the bathroom door, not at the foot of the bed. Ideally the wall the strings run toward, so a card and the altar sit in the same line of sight.

Incense: thin low-smoke sandalwood joss sticks, three at a time. Because cats are in the house, treat real incense as the exception and the red electric candles as the default. Burn incense only with the window open and the cats out of the room, in a session of one stick's length, and stop at the first sneeze. A single beeswax candle is the middle option when the cats are elsewhere.

## The weekly act

Pick one evening and keep it. About ten minutes.

1. Change the water. Put a piece of fruit on the plate if you have one.
2. Light the candles (switch on the electric pair, or light the real one).
3. Stand facing the strings. Say your name, the date, and that this is the week's thanks.
4. Name, aloud, anything that moved this week: a wish that progressed, a wish that landed, a kindness received. Gratitude first, before any new ask.
5. Read the cards you can see, each one once, without comment.
6. If a wish has been fulfilled this week, do the fulfilment step below now.
7. If incense is in use: light three sticks from the candle, wave the flame out, hold them at brow height with both hands, bow once, plant them centre, left, right.
8. Sit or stand with the cards for as long as feels right, then switch off the candles, or leave the electric pair on overnight.

## Spontaneous moments

When a card catches your eye in passing, stop for the length of one breath, read it, and say "thank you" or the wish itself, silently or aloud. Nothing more. This is the feeding. It is not Chinese, and it does not need to be.

## When a wish is fulfilled

The repayment is what keeps the practice honest, so do it within the week.

1. Say the wish and its fulfilment aloud at the altar, with thanks.
2. Give the gift of thanks. The traditional forms are fruit or flowers on the plate, a donation to a cause in the area of the wish, or an act of kindness done in its name. Pick one each time and name it aloud.
3. Move the card from its string to a dedicated thanks line nearest the altar, so the fulfilled wishes gather in one place through the year.

## When a wish is let go

Take the card down, say that you release it, and put it in the New Year box. It burns with the rest.

## The New Year burn

On or near 6 February 2027, and annually after:

1. Take every card down, thanks line first.
2. Read each fulfilled card aloud with thanks. Read each open card aloud and either release it or rewrite it fresh on a new card.
3. Burn the old cards outdoors in a fire-safe container, with thanks spoken once over the fire. The burning is the delivery.
4. Set out fresh water, fruit and flowers. Hang the first new cards, and leave the candles on through the Lantern Festival (20 February 2027).

## What to buy

- Red cotton cord, 2 to 3 mm, and small wall hooks.
- Red or gold card stock, a hole punch, red string, small S-hooks.
- A small ceramic bowl and a bag of clean sand, or a joss-stick holder.
- A pair of red electric altar candles (battery or mains).
- A small plate and a small cup, ideally ceramic.
- Thin low-smoke sandalwood joss sticks.
- A fire-safe metal container for the New Year burn.
